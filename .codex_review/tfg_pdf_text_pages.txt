Abstract
This project investigates how cinema can be used as a cultural lens to study the evolution of society
across decades through the analysis of movie scripts using Large Language Models(LLMs).
The study is based on a dataset of more than 1250 movie scripts from multiple periods, providing a large
view of different narrative, thematic, and social patterns. To contribute to this objective, the project
combines prompt engineering and LLMs to extract structured and defined information from screenplays
in a consistent way.
The analytical framework includes narrative and genre variables, technical features, social and cultural
patterns, historical references, and thematic features relevant to social change. To conduct the analysis,
the creation of a custom processing pipeline is needed, including GPU workers to run the local model
and validation strategies to improve the JSON output. This pipeline implements a hybrid feature
extraction, combining LLM based semantic analysis with deterministic methods such as regular
expressions and coding-based counting and parsing.
By aggregating the extracted features across decades, the projects seek to identify how cinema reflects
the changes in social movements, ideologies, and tendences over time.
Overall, this project presents movie scripts as a valuable object for studying the evolution of society and
also explores the ability of LLM in structured long text analysis.

Chapter 1
Introduction
This project is based on the idea that Cinema may be viewed as a cultural reflection. Thus, Films are
not only vehicles for storytelling, but they are reflective of the values, struggles and concerns of the
societies that create them.
This Study uses a combination of a cultural approach and computational analysis by utilizing a large
dataset of Movie Scripts processed with Large Language Models to investigate if historical and social
trends can be identified through repeated patterns in film narratives, characters, themes and
representations.
1.1 Motivation and Context
Cinema has always been more than entertainment: it is a mirror of society. Through characters and
stories, cinema captures social values, moral codes, cultural treats and stereotypes of the historical
period it was produced. By observing how cinema evolves across the decades, we can follow the
transformation in gender roles, political ideologies, discourse, and cultural structures. Films are an
invaluable archive of human thought and emotion, one that reflects social change.
Thanks to the digital era, we can now analyze cinema easily. Thousands of scripts from different decades
are available online. However, traditional film analysis is usually focused on individual works, directors,
or movements, which makes it difficult to see large-scale trends. Thanks to the arrival of Artificial
Intelligence tools, especially Large Language Models (LLMs), we can explore these scripts in a new
way. These models can recognize ideas and narrative patterns that repeat or change over time, helping
us understand how films reflect the evolution of society.
My motivation behind this topic also comes from my passion for cinema. Movies have always fascinated
me, not only as entertainment, but to understand people, cultures and historical moments. This curiosity
is what inspired me to combine my love for cinema with my knowledge in technology. With this, the
project seeks to explore how the stories told on screen evolve at the same time as the society that creates
them.
In the end, this project tries to show how technology can also be used to analyze culture and art. It shows
how artificial intelligence, which is usually used in technical or scientific fields, can help us look at
creative works from another perspective. By applying computational tools to film scripts, this project
hopes to connect objective data analysis and human creativity, showing how both can work together to
better understand the society we live in.
1.2 Objectives
The primary goal of the research project is to investigate whether cinema can represent a reflection of
societal changes based upon a large-scale analysis of movie scripts. Specifically, the project will
develop an analytical framework that allows for the identification of narrative, social, cultural and
historical aspects of movies with Large Language Models (LLMs) within a structured process for
analyzing a very large number of movie scripts.
There are four goals associated with achieving this. First, the development of a wide and diverse data
set of movie scripts representing various decades in cinema history. Second, the creation of a

consistent workflow that integrates LLM-based semantic extraction with predetermined rules and
deterministic calculations. Third, the transformation of all extracted information into a structured
format that facilitates comparative analyses across films and overtime. Fourth, to use visual
explorations to detect trends regarding representation, institutions, conflict, technologies and other
sociological aspects that are representative of social changes throughout time.
Therefore, while the ultimate purpose of the project is to examine films as individual cultural objects,
the project also aims to position these objects in the context of the historical moments and social

1.3 State of the Art
The connection between cinema and society has been extensively researched. Many view films not only
as art or business profit, but as cultural records that document values, conflicts and ideologies of the
time in which they were produced. Now, with the increasing digitalization of media and the rise of
LLMs, there have been greater opportunities to study cinema on a larger scale.
Additionally, recent advancements in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Large Language Models
have provided researchers with additional opportunities for examining lengthy and complex texts.
Traditional computational approaches frequently rely on keywords or metadata. However, LLMs allow
researchers to analyze at large scale and with much more detail.
Despite this potential, most studies continue to focus on reviews, subtitles or shorter forms of textual
representations of films as opposed to full-length movie scripts. Consequently, this research project is
situated in the middle of film analysis and applied artificial intelligence, proposing a script-based
methodology utilizing LLMs to extract structural conclusions about what cinematic representations
indicate concerning social evolution over time.

Background And Methodology
2.1 Dataset Construction
Cinema is a vast field, and to be able to do a meaningful analysis about how society changes through
films, we need access to a large amount of data. The first challenge was collecting a comprehensive and
extensive dataset of movie scripts that cover different decades of cinema history. This section explains
how we created the dataset of 1257 movie scripts, making it possible to analyze how society appears in
cinema across more than a century.
We began with an existing public dataset from HuggingFace, which contained 700+ scripts (anhnv125,
n.d). While this provided a good starting point, we needed more scripts and more variety to achieve our
objectives. So we decided to add more scripts to create a more complete dataset by combining the
existing dataset with new scripts obtained from web scraping IMSDb (Internet Movie Script Database),
one of the largest repositories of free available movie scripts.
To collect and download the new scripts, we developed a Python web scraper to automatically go
through the script list on IMSDb. For every movie script on the website, we checked if the script was
already in the dataset. If it was not the case, we downloaded the new script and extracted the raw text
and movie title and inserted them into the dataset.
The final step was organizing the scripts into decades. This was not a necessity but helped us to have
more visual feedback on the dataset, and allowed us to see a very important aspect of it: how well
distributed it was. Sadly, we found out that our dataset was not evenly distributed, due to two main
reasons. First, digital scripts have become increasingly available as a major effort for digitalization.
Second, due to the growth of the film industry itself, every year producing more movies, and hence,
more scripts.
In the end, we have a dataset of 1257 scripts, distributed from the 1910s to the 2020s. These scripts are
stored as 2 columns: one for the title, and the other for the whole script in plain text.
2.2 Large Language Models
The analysis of movie scripts at scale requires advanced natural language processing. Large Language
Models (LLMs) have emerged as powerful tools for understanding and extracting information from
texts, which is why they’re the perfect tool for this project.
The world of LLMs has grown significantly in recent years, with more powerful, accurate and accessible
models coming out every day, so it is very important to choose the right model for our study. When
selecting the LLM that will be used, we need to have several factors considered: the price per token,
context window, reasoning capabilities, and most importantly: whether to use a cloud model or a local
model.

2.2.1 Cloud Models vs Local Models
The deployment of AI models presents two different paradigms: the execution in remote servers (Cloud
Models) and the execution in the user’s hardware or a virtual machine (Local Models). Choosing
between one or another of these paradigms depends on the technical scope of the project, and
requirements such as hardware limitations, data privacy or scalability.
Cloud models, such as the ones accessible through external APIs or through the provider’s website, are
hosted in datacenters. Their principal advantage is that these clusters can host the most powerful models,
allowing for huge context windows and billions of parameters, resulting in better performance and
outputs. The main problem is that the usage of these models is often paid, with varying prices per 1M
tokens depending on the model’s capability.
On the other hand, Local models are executed directly in the user’s machine. They stand out by their
privacy, and the ability to modify parameters or characteristics of the model, as well as being able to
train or fine-tune the models. Also, Local models are usually free to use, so you will only have to pay
for the hardware needed to deploy the model.

2.3 Analysis Strategies
This section focuses on the methodology used to analyze the 1200+ scripts of the dataset. The goal is to
extract meaningful patterns that reflect how cinema mirrors society’s changes across decades. We will
do an analysis strategy that examines script from four different perspectives:
 Narrative and genre characteristics (19 features): this block contains features that describe the
plot, its central characters and overall dramatic tone: primary genre, opening type, protagonist
gender...
 Technical and structural elements (8 features): this block captures measurable screenplay
characteristics, including size, and scene composition among others (Scene count, INT/EXT
ratio, dialogue density %...)
 Social and cultural themes (34 features): this section covers representation, sexuality, race,
class, family dynamics, violence, morality, science, and institutions (Bechdel Test, LGBTQ
presence, Sexual content presence...)
 Connections to real-world historical events (6 features). This section captures direct links
between the screenplay and named wars, historical events, and social movements. (War
reference, War portrayal, social movement focus...)
With a total of 67 features to analyze, it is very important that the result is consistent across all the
scripts. That is why we defined a finite set of possible responses in every feature, so the model must
choose the one that fits the best, without generating answers outside the set. (See Appendix B)
Once all the blocks and features we wanted to analyze were defined, and the dataset was constructed,
we could move to the next phase: build an optimized framework to conduct the analysis. This includes
constructing a prompt, building the execution pipeline and configuring the model.

Chapter 3
Implementation and Analysis Methodology
In this Chapter, we will discuss in detail the practical aspects of running the Script Analysis with the
help of Large Language Models (LLMs). We will outline each stage of the way in which we have
processed the scripts dataset using LLMs, starting at the setup of the model within the Python notebook,
through to the initial prompt creation through to the result of the processing of the data.
3.1 Prompt Designing, Development and Structure
Prompt design or "prompt engineering" refers to creating prompts that are clear and instructive enough
to enable models to consistently generate results that meet the intended specifications. Commonly used
prompt design techniques include specifying a role for the model, breaking processes into sequential
steps, and defining an expected output.
As previously mentioned, LLM responses are indeterminate. Therefore, when analyzing two different
versions of the same script, there is no guarantee that you will obtain identical results. Therefore, our
prompt designs were created to be as consistent as possible across all scripts to make comparisons of
results viable. (See Appendix D).
To be able to communicate correctly with the model, and following the prompt engineering theory, we
divided the prompts into different sections or components to ensure the model has as much context as
possible and the instructions and definitions are as explicit as possible. The prompt components are Role
assignment, Task Definition, Feature Instructions and Category Constraints (See Appendix C for more
detail).
After having the initial prompt, we had to test it and tune it based on the model’s reaction to that prompt.
Prompt development is an important step in developing quality prompts. We used an iterative approach
while developing the prompts, testing the model's responses to the prompts, and evaluating those
responses. As the prompts were developed, we would refine them based on the response received from
the model. We kept iterating until we got the desired results and thought it was not possible to obtain
more accuracy.
At the end, the obtained prompt system doesn’t consist of a single instruction set or a single piece, it
works like a multi-stage pipe consisting of three parts of the prompt: a stateless chunk extraction part, a
separate Bechdel Test evaluation part, and the last part which is a core analyzer.
3.2 Implementation Core
This section defines how the analysis process was implemented technically after the criteria and
prompting structures have been defined. In this section, the execution environment is described,
including the model used in the project, required libraries for running this model, the runtime
configuration parameters, and the strategy employed for executing models in parallel.
Our implementation was created with Python using a Jupyter Notebook via Kaggle. In order to run the
chosen model, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct-AWQ, we had to make use of an inference framework, tensor data
management and other libraries.
As said before, the use of a local mode requires a lot of tweaking and configuration to run the model
properly. We spent a lot of time testing with different context windows, temperature and other factors

until speed, performance and quality were the desired. (See Appendix E for more information in

In order to speed up processing times across thousands of scripts, the design of the execution architecture

3.3 Dataset Preparation
In order to feed the unprocessed text into the LLM, the source data required proper preprocessing. The
preprocessing phase included cleaning and normalizing the data to ensure all film titles corresponded to
only one valid script. It is by eliminating duplicates and preventing invalid input that preprocessing
avoids errors.
Initially the source dataset was made up of around 700 scripts drawn from an open public dataset found
at Hugging Face. In addition to increasing the historical scope of the dataset, we extended this original
dataset using web scraping techniques to obtain more scripts from IMSdb. Therefore, the resulting
dataset reached 1256 film scripts. As noted above, the dataset consisted of only two columns: Title,
Script.
Combining datasets from different sources inevitably results in duplicate entries. For dealing with
duplicate scripts, we used a straightforward elimination method based on the Title field. When a film
title was encountered more than once, we compared the length of the entries and kept the longer version.
It is reasonable to assume that larger files typically correspond to the complete or the definitive version
of the script, so we were able to reject early drafts or incomplete versions.
Movie scripts obtained from the internet often contain inconsistent formatting, hidden characters, or
unnecessary blank spaces. To ensure that the text could be processed consistently by the extraction
pipeline, we applied a basic normalization step. This process included removing unnecessary
whitespace, standardizing line breaks, and eliminating unusual characters that could interfere with the
model’s behavior or affect tokenization. Thanks to this, we could also reduce the size of each script in
tokens, allowing the LLM to process scripts more quickly.
Information beyond the actual screenplay can help identify and analyze historical trends. Therefore,
metadata fields such as the film title, the release year and decade, and the script source have been
obtained. When executed, the metadata variables associated with each field were sent straight to the
pipeline so the model could include the metadata in the final structured JSON.
3.4 Multi-stage Data Analysis Pipeline
With the data cleaned and standardized, we could already proceed to the next step. However, extracting
complex social or historical attributes from an entire screenplay will be very difficult using one pass,
primarily because of the limited size of the model and its context window, which led to a well-known
problem called the “lost in the middle” problem, in which LLMs tend to lose track of retrieving and
using information located in the middle of a long input or context. To correct this issue, a multistage
data analysis pipeline was created. The multistage method divides the overall task into multiple small
tasks: First, a stage of fact-based evidence was identified from each chunk of the script and returned in

--- PAGE 20 ---
8
an intermediate output, which is passed into the next chunk for context. Second, the individual outputs
are combined to create a complete analysis of the entire film.
This project is based on a multistage pipeline architecture that transforms large amounts of raw,
unstructured screenplay text into a very structured JSON format. The first stage in this process is the
preprocessing phase, in which the screenplays are cleaned up (for example, removing formatting), and
matched with additional external metadata from the TMDB API. These two pieces of information will
provide both chronological and thematic context for each screenplay. Once the text has been
preprocessed, it is sent through the next stage called extraction. In the extraction phase the screenplay
text is broken down into smaller sections (chunks) and passed to an LLM for processing using a custom
"stateless" schema to capture raw evidence. Following the extraction stage is a validation/verification
layer which includes a specialized sub-pipeline for the Bechdel Test, which was very problematic. The
purpose of this layer is to eliminate false positives and ensure data accuracy. Finally, there is an
aggregate step that combines results from all the previous steps into one comprehensive global analysis
of the movie. This final analysis is then converted into the JSON output.
Since the length movie scripts is generally quite long, and because there are limits to how much context
an LLM can be aware of when processing a piece of text at once, we developed a chunking strategy.
Rather than try to get the LLM to process an entire screenplay in one pass, which would result in "lost
in the middle" problems and much slower analysis, we divide each script into segments of around 7000
characters. This allows us to keep the amount of content the model focuses on per pass extremely low
while also giving us enough material to analyze individual scenes and lines of dialogue with sufficient
detail so that the model can extract reliable information. Even though problems related to long context
windows were not possible now, there were still risks associated with losing some of the information
near the edges of the segments. To help prevent losing important details we defined an overlap area of
120 characters between each segment. This overlap is very important in preserving the continuity of the
narrative. Without an overlap area, if we cut off a line of dialogue in the middle or leave part of a
description of a scene without its final sentence, it could lose relevance or completely distort the
significance of what was said. By providing the model with a small window of both preceding and
following text, we ensured that our pipeline preserved all contextual information that spanned across
multiple segments and allowed our extraction process to remain consistent through the entire script.
Unlike other approaches which force the LLM to classify things immediately after analyzing a section
of text, our pipeline uses an intermediate “stateless” extraction method. We break each segment down
into independent signals of evidence such as potential Bechdel interactions, explicit historical
references, or social issue indicators, without allowing the model to know anything about what comes
before or after. This approach keeps the model from making assumptions about how events have
developed in the story based on nothing but superficial observations about what has happened in each
segment. Instead, we allow our model to identify concrete evidence directly related to what is found
within each individual chunk.
3.5 Chunk Level Evidence Extraction
At this level, the system makes no claims about the film's overall themes or plot. The focus here is to
extract short, fragmented, and fact-based evidence at the local level from each segment. The reason for
this focus is to limit the possibility of the model creating a large-scale theme and to ensure all claims
made are supported by direct quotes of specific conversations or descriptions of individual scenes.
Chunk-level extraction was used to detect representation-related evidence, social and institutional
signals, historical and political references and violence, sexuality and contextual features. (See
Appendix F for more details in the Evidence Extraction of each feature group)
To ensure the LLM follows these strict rules, the extraction is done with a structured schema, where
each chunk generates an intermediate JSON containing signals and evidence of that specific chunk
only, allowing them to aggregate the signals and evidence of all chunks to construct the final JSON.

3.6 Script Level Data Synthesis and Feature Integration
This step combines the individual chunk data into one single script report. A single output will be
produced per individual chunk (as described above) and this output cannot be used on its own. Rather
it will be integrated into an aggregation of evidence structure so that the model has a full view of the
film. The evidence structure created at the end of the pipeline will be combined with the previously
calculated deterministic metrics to create the final prompt to produce the full JSON analysis for each
film.
To prevent duplication and allow for easier tracking of the overall storyline, evidence collected from all
chunks (individual dialogue pieces, scenes, etc.) will be combined into aggregate lists. For example, if
there are multiple references to the same character or event within multiple individual chunks, they will
be consolidated. Therefore, the final analysis will be completed with a completely deduped set of
evidence representing the entire film, rather than an aggregation of disparate snapshots.
As previously mentioned, while the LLM handles the semantic analysis of the script content, the pipeline
also calculates various deterministic features using Python. The purpose of creating these deterministic
features is to provide a measurable, objective framework against which the results of the semantic
analysis can be compared. The features include Word Count, Scene Count or Dialogue Density among
others. By calculating these features using Python, we ensure that all technical aspects of the feature
extraction process have been done 100% accurately, and we also take off load from the model, in
features that can be calculated with precision without need.
Then, a detailed analysis of the script’s characters is done by identifying speakers and estimating their
gender. Additionally, the system identifies and measures participation levels for each character by
mapping speaker names to their corresponding estimated gender. This analysis includes measuring "Top
Speakers," and "dialogue share" percentages for male and female characters. While informative, these
measurements are more importantly utilized by the model as "supporting signals" to help in its
interpretation of power dynamics and lead structures when classifying films during the final
classification phase.
To provide additional support to the LLM's final classification decision, the notebook uses a variety of
rule-based pre-checks. These pre-checks utilize both keyword searches and regular expressions to
identify indicators of key structural and thematic elements found within screenplays. These pre-checks
are used as a safety net, providing clear indication to the final prompt that certain themes exist in a
screenplay and therefore reducing the possibility that an element may be overlooked. (See Appendix H
for more information).
The last step of the pipeline is the creation of the Final Core Prompt. This final core prompt incorporates
all information from previous steps (aggregated evidence, Python metrics, speaker analysis, prechecks),
which then enables the LLM to generate a valid JSON object that adheres strictly to the
CORE_FINAL_SCHEMA. As a result, this final object will contain a definitive classification of all
relevant dimensions of a film, ensuring that all features are consistent in format and prepared for
analysis. (See Appendix K for more information)
3.7 Post-Processing and Validation
Following the generation of the final output from the LLM, the model’s output is not accepted blindly.
Since it has been demonstrated that models may sometimes make over-inferences, as well as be
subjected to the influence of their own training bias. The verification section will provide corrections

--- PAGE 22 ---
10
and cross-reference the LLMs’ narrative conclusions to hard data produced in the pre-processor and
chunk sections of the pipeline, providing an additional level of accuracy.
To ensure data quality, we developed a verification logic phase for some problematic features. These
verification logic phases are applied for the following: Bechdel Test, LGBTQ representation,
protagonist gender and lead structure , racial/minority representation and institution/ historical.
In each of these phases, the payload provided by the model is verified to ensure no false positives are
being marked. Thanks to this, we can detect moments when the model hallucinates or when it does not
understand completely the context, for example categorizing the protagonist as black because in the
script it says, “black t-shirt". (See Appendix I for more information)
The fundamental design decision across all steps of the pipeline has been accuracy above speculation
and guessing. Therefore, in situations where there is an ambiguous narrative or evidence, the system is
designed to default to unknown, none, or false classifications rather than arbitrarily selecting one from
among many possibilities. As such, the conservative nature of the pipeline minimizes noise in the
resultant dataset and allows for reliable comparisons to be made.
3.8 Output Format and Creating Final Datasets
In this last stage of the process, we describe the change from raw analysis to creating structural data. In
this step, we seek to merge all the evidence and validated measures into one single structure that is both
readable by machines and, more importantly, understandable to humans and therefore capable of being
compared and used to extract conclusions.
For each movie script we create a comprehensive JSON object that follows our
CORE_FINAL_SCHEMA. Our core_schema represents the standard definition of the movie within the
study, while structuring our information along six fundamental categories: title/year/ID,
script/dialogue/scenes, character/representation, social conflict/institution/technology/events and
themes (See appendix J for more information)
To make sure our pipeline was not simply a black box, we included an evidence payload section in our
JSON. Each piece of evidence, including the exact dialogues, candidate events and snippets that
supported each conclusion are stored here. So, when we store our results, the entire trail of validation is
retained so if we decide later to revisit some decisions, we can immediately see which lines in the
screenplay created that outcome.
We built our final structured outputs with visual analytics in mind. We've made sure that our output
contains clean categorical and numeric variables suitable for use in data analytics libraries. To reach our
goal of showing how representations of social issues, technology and gender have changed in cinema
since its beginning, we need to convert a dataset of screenplays into a quantifiable map of social history.

4.2 Gender Representation: Visibility, Participation and Influence on the

A key dimension for this study is gender representation. Cinema transmits social attitudes about gender
through its characters and narratives. Therefore, the number of women present in each screenplay
represents how women's roles have been represented over time. Gender representation is measured in
this study (in addition to simply counting the number of female characters) through evaluating women's
percentage of dialogue, how often they serve as main characters, and if the movie meets the Bechdel
test criteria.
The results illustrate a positive trend in women's representation over the last several decades. Although
films do not yet depict men and women equally, there appears to be an improvement in writing women
into film scripts. In many earlier decades, the average female dialogue share remained at or near 20-
25% while during the 2010 decade, it increased to 28.2% and during the 2020 decade, it reached 35.4%.
The increase is obvious but far from an equitable split.

4.5 Violence, Darkness and Morality
Another way to view cinema as a reflection of social change is by looking at tone. Cinema's depiction
of social change does not simply manifest itself in characters. Rather, it can be seen in the overall mood
or ethic of the movies. Darkness and moral ambiguity are two of the most effective ways to measure
this change, and are useful for determining how film perceives conflict, authority, fear and human
behavior in different historical contexts.
The data shows an increase in violence as the decades go by, particularly when looking at earlier decades
(1930s) to later decades (1980s-90s). In the early 30's, there was an average violence intensity of 2.08.
By the 70's this had increased to approximately 3.00 and had been the largest value for all the data points
since then. After that and through the 80's, 90's and 2000's, violence intensity remained higher than the
preceding decades' averages, but dropped slightly in the 10's and 20's. Therefore, it appears as if violence
began to be more visible or socially acceptable within the context of films throughout those periods in
history that are associated with war memories, social unrest in cities, loss of faith in institutions and
general societal unrest.

trafficking, nightlife settings or substance-related actions or behaviors are appearing increasingly
commonly. Furthermore, this could relate to many of the same social issues, for example the 1960s

awareness of addiction as a both personal issue and a structural problem by the public.

Film has rarely used drug culture as an indifferent element. Instead, drugs have been associated with
rebellion, marginality, pleasure, trauma and economic necessity. The prevalence of drug culture in the
dataset suggests that films frequently portray society as excessive, unstable and escapist.
In combination, the findings indicate that cinema tends to represent society as being at tension rather
than calm. As such, violence increases in visibility, dark tones continue to dominate film throughout
much of the last century, and morality increasingly loses its clear distinctions as films move further
away from simple portrayals of good vs. evil. The results clearly demonstrate that there does not exist a
direct relationship between specific historical events and specific films. However, the findings do clearly
illustrate how cinema responds to social attitudes toward issues such as war, distrust, inequality and
cultural division.
Therefore, violence and darkness can be understood as indicators of the cultural climate of the time in
which the films were made. Film absorbs the fears of its time. As film becomes darker, more violent
and more morally ambiguous, it typically indicates a society re-examining itself and its beliefs about
authority, safety, individuality and the existence of clear moral answers. (See Appendix L.5)

4.6 Institutions, Power and Distrust
Cinema reflects society through both its depiction of characters and themes. However, cinema also
reflects society by the way it depicts institutions. Institutional representations (governments,
corporations, law enforcement...) are depicted in film as more than just an environment for plot
development. The institutional elements create conflicts in the story and provide insight into what type
of power structure each era views as the source of legitimate authority. Therefore, institutional
representation is particularly significant in a study of cinema as a reflection of societal change.
The results presented below show that overall institutional negativism was the single largest and most
consistent signal within our data set. As such, many films depict institutions as corrupting influences,
limiting factors or a source of conflict rather than as neutral entities providing protection. This trend
clearly began to emerge during the mid-20th Century. It has been evident since then that cinema is
becoming increasingly skeptical of governmental, corporate and judicial authority.
Figure 4.20: Graph showing institution negativity portrayal by decade
This trend may be related to larger, historical trends. For many societies during the latter half of the 20th
Century, there were events that challenged confidence in government: wars, scandals, corrupt
governments, governmental oppression and financial crises. The Cold War, Vietnam and Watergate, for
instance, all led to increased distrust of the state in the U.S. Beyond this, later modernity increasingly
conceived of institutions as being not just organizational structures governing public life but as
organizations with potential to either watch over their citizens (surveillance) or control them. Cinema
has reflected these changes by placing institutions in an obviously negative/antagonistic role.
Police should receive special emphasis. In many films, police officers are not represented simply to
protect people and maintain order. Instead, they are portrayed as absent, ineffective, abusive or corrupt.
That is important because it typically exposes film audiences to images of law enforcement being an
element of the problem (as opposed to an element of stability), creating a narrative universe where
justice is unattainable and institutional legitimacy has been erased.

One of the most interesting cross-results is the correlation of film portrayals of police with racial

It is stronger in those narratives which represent societal conflict.
These results can also be associated with significant social activities such as civil rights movements,
arguments about police violence, public scandals, business corruption and the growth of surveillance.
This all helps define a greater awareness of institutional power within contemporary culture. Although
cinema does not represent these realities in a direct way it is able to transform them into a narrative
where institutions are frequently untrustworthy.
In general, the results illustrate that many modern films imagine their societies by the conflict between
an individual's desire for freedom vs. societal systems of authority. Institutions are not just a component
of the environment of a film, institutions serve as representations of the concerns, inequality and conflict
within the society that creates them. In this manner, institutional representation is one of the most
obvious ways in which cinema represents social transformation in how people think politically and have
confidence in institutions. (See Appendix L.6)

4.8 Historical Memory, War and Political Context
Film, as well as reflecting the time during which it was produced, will revisit the past. Cinema will retell
the story of wars or crisis as narratives. For this reason, historical and political references were
particularly interesting for this project because they demonstrate how film preserves collective memory
and converts real events into cultural stories.
Historical/political references are evident throughout the dataset. The overall number of
historical/political references contained within the data set, when compared to all films (filtered) is
37.9%. Historical/political references have remained consistent from decade to decade, with the highest
numbers found within the 1950s, 1970s, 1990s, 2000s and 2020s. This evidence demonstrates that while
film has returned to history numerous times, this return is not simply an occasional occurrence. Instead,
it appears that history continues to be a source of long-term narrative material.
One of the clearest examples of this is war. War references are apparent in 28.0% of the filtered films.
Historically, there was a continued level of representation of war references throughout multiple
decades. These levels included: 29.8% in the 1970s, 29.6% in the 1980s, 30.2% in the 1990s and 30.1%
in the 2010s. The continued use of references to war indicates that the conflict is not limited to those
years in which the conflict occurred. Rather, the representation of violence in film seems to be more
related to a continuing process of remembering, evaluating and debating violence.

The three biggest war-related signals come from:
 Vietnam (with an occurrence rate of 9.2%),
 WWII (with an occurrence rate of 5.4%)
 Cold War (with an occurrence rate of 4.9%).
All three have had a major impact on the public psyche: Vietnam was seen as a representation of the
American people's loss of faith in politics, WWII has been the most important example of a national
traumatic event in the last century, and the Cold War has represented the decades-long unease with
which Americans lived during that time
Film shows us that while cinema may entertain, it is also a means for societies to deal with unresolved
historical conflicts and tensions.
Although political climate references occur less frequently than the previous examples, there are still

prevalent in our data set. Although other politically based labels, such as post-9/11 Terrorism, and

Additionally, historical significance allows for further refinement of this analysis. Historical events are

each contributed to the cultural atmosphere in which films were produced. While films do not directly
mirror their respective cultural contexts, they do translate those cultures into individual narratives,
symbolic battles, and emotional realities. Thus, films serve as a form of cultural archives recording both
actual historical events and how those events are were felt and perceived by the society in which the
film was created.
4.9 Limitations
Although the results provide great insight into how cinema depicts societal change and what trends exist,
we must have some caution when interpreting these findings. While the size of the data set allows us to
see the big trends, we cannot assume equal representation across all years of cinematic history. There
were significantly more scripts produced during certain decades (such as the 1990s, 2000s and 2010s)
than there were during other decades. Therefore, we can feel somewhat safer in drawing conclusions
about the last three decades of cinema than we can with the first half of the 20th Century.
A second limitation exists with respect to the original source materials upon which this analysis is
founded. We used available movie scripts, not completed movies. Thus, while some movie scripts may
differ from their corresponding finished film, other scripts may exhibit format issues, missing scenes,
or truncated dialogue. While our filtering removes obviously low-quality records, we cannot assure that
each and every script will be similarly complete and faithful to the final movie cut.
Similarly, using Large Language Models introduces additional limitations. Although our pipeline was
designed to minimize the potential for errors by being conservative and well structured, an LLM can
miss implicit references or misinterpret the ambiguity differently than how a human would. This is
particularly true for themes such as race, representations of LGBTQ+ and historical references where
our systems rely solely upon explicit textual evidence and hence may not capture indirect expressions
of an issue.
Overall, these limitations do not invalidate the results, but they define how they should be read. There
are obviously some low-quality features whose results may not be taken as seriously as others. This was
expected, since every feature has its own difficulties and some of them couldn’t be handled correctly by
the pipeline. Apart from that, we think the results and the dashboard are a very good tool for exploring
cultural tendencies in scripts, even though they cannot be used as a definitive measurement of cinema.
(See Appendix L.5)

The overall results of this chapter support the main idea of the project: cinema can be studied as a
cultural mirror of society. Even though it does not show a complete reflection of reality, it does reveal
meaningful patterns about how films represent social change across decades. It shows how cultural
concerns are transformed into stories, through gender, identity, class, violence, institutions, technology
and history.

An important area covered in this project relates to the representation of women and minority groups
in film. Female dialogue share or Bechdel Test pass rates have been increasing since the 1930s,
especially in the last few decades. The same applies to the inclusion of minorities in film today, with
LGBTQ+ representation and identity related themes having more visibility. Although these
improvements are present and they suggest that cinema has become more open, the results also clearly
indicate that there still needs to be improvement made to provide true diversity in film.
Another topic discussed relates to the ability of film to reflect society. While film does reflect society,
it does so in a unique manner. Film takes on social issues, like economic struggle, violence,
institutional distrust or fear of technology and converts them into visual representations of conflict.
Each issue mentioned above is used by filmmakers to create a sense of anxiety within the audience. In
addition to creating anxiety within the audience, filmmakers use these issues to allow audiences to see
themselves reflected in what they watch.
The work demonstrates that Large Language Models can provide important assistance in studying
culture when asked with careful design of prompts, and when the output is validated and interpreted
cautiously. This is not because such models will replace the judgment of humans, but rather because
they help to identify trends and relationships which could never be identified by hand for more than a
thousand films. Therefore, it seems that such models act less as the "final word" on whether something
is true, and more like a tool to organize evidence and compare films and their tendency over time.
In addition to demonstrating the potential of using such models for analyzing culture, the project also
illustrates the need for limits. The meaning of culture is ambiguous. Therefore, a machine can never
completely understand all ambiguities, silences, or implied references contained in a script. Therefore,
the output generated by this process should not be viewed as definitive truths about cinema, but instead
as organized data/evidence that can be used as part of an interpretive framework. When used in a
thoughtful manner, this approach offers an interesting way to combine computational approaches to
analyzing media texts with traditional humanities approaches to understanding how society imagines
itself through its movies.

Appendix A: Chosen Model technical information
The chosen model was Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct-AWQ, we chose it because of its balance between

● 7B (7 billion) parameters: this means that the neural network of the model has 7 billion

Appendix C: Detailed Prompt Structure
This is an outline of the structure of the prompt used in the pipeline, the major parts include:
1. Role assignment: The first line of every prompt assigns the model to a particular role ("You are
an evidence extractor for screening play analysis."), which restricts its behavior and causes the
model to treat the input as if it were just data to analyze.
2. Task Definition: Following the establishment of the role, the prompt defines what the model
needs to do. For example, the prompt used to determine evidence at the chunk level instructs
the model to "extract only textual or near-textual evidence from that fragment," and specifies
that the model should not use the evidence contained within a single scene to classify the entire
script.
3. Feature Instructions: Because certain features (such as race or sexuality) contain nuances
requiring interpretation, the prompt contains definitions regarding how to interpret these
features. In reference to evidence concerning LGBTQ characteristics, the prompt allows the
model to identify only “serious” and “explicit” mentions of these characteristics, and does not
allow it to count jokes, insults, sexual assault, coercion or vague context. When evaluating the
Bechdel Test, the model is required to evaluate whether there are two named women present in
the script, and exclude generic names like “waitress”,”nurse”, etc.
4. Category Constraints: as previously stated, the output is dependent on pre-defined categories
which will facilitate statistical analysis of the output. Therefore, the prompt contains constraints
related to category output: “Produce one valid JSON that strictly follows the schema”, including
enumerations of acceptable values for each feature, defined in CORE_FINAL_SCHEMA.
Missing Information Handling: generative models can produce hallucinations due to missing evidence.
The prompts have been designed to be restrictive: therefore, they require the model to generate either
an empty list or “none/unknown” if evidence is insufficient or non-existent.
Appendix D: Prompt Constraints and Output Formatting.
To develop the prompt, the factors to be analyzed (described in Chapter 2.3) were identified: they were
organized into an appropriate format, e.g., Boolean and ordinal scales. Organizing these on a similar
scale allowed for consistent returns from the model when analyzing all the scripts. An example of this
was when measuring the level of drama within a script. We determined there would be a 1-to-5 scale to
measure the level of drama in each script. This ensured that no matter what type of feedback was
provided (i.e. "very dramatic", etc.) the model would provide a value for the level of drama in that range,
allowing us to easily make comparisons.
Once all the possible responses for each feature were defined, the next step was to define the output
format. To simplify the conversion of the models' results into graphical data using Python libraries, we

--- PAGE 52 ---
40
determined to use the exact same JSON format for all scripts, with the only field populated being the
field that corresponded to the specific output.
Finally, the base prompt was constructed. This contained the task definition, descriptions of each
analysis factor, specifications of the output format, definitions of categories and examples. Additionally,
we included specifications for how missing information was handled and defined the expected JSON
schema that the model should produce.
Appendix E: Notebook and Model Configuration Details
● Inference Framework: due to compatibility issues with the available GPU resources, as well as
its high-performance capabilities at an acceptable speed level; vLLM was selected as the
inference framework. As a result, both Hugging Face Transformers and PyTorch were used for
model load operations and tensor data management.
● AWQ Optimization: By utilizing a 4-bit quantified version of the model and employing
activation aware weight quantization (AWQ), VRAM utilization was greatly reduced while
very little loss in precision occurred.
● Data Management: Libraries like Pandas were used to manipulate datasets. JSON was used to
create a structured format prior to storage for the model's responses.
Due to almost all scripts being longer than the context window, we set for the model, we chose to
implement a sliding-window segmentation strategy.
In addition to chunking (described above), the following parameters were also defined as part of the
configuration:
● Determinism: we used strict generation parameters to ensure the same output for the same script
every time. The Generation Temperature is an example of this where it was set very close to
zero. This ensured the least amount of randomness occurred during generation.
● State and recovery system: due to the high volume of scripts needed to be processed, there was
a saving logic implemented. Before analyzing a new script, each worker would check the output
folder for analysis results. If results already existed for that script, they would skip on to the
next script. This feature was very useful, especially due to Kaggle’s 12-hour session limit.
Appendix F: Extraction Rules of Different Feature Categories
All categories were extracted using conservative evidence-first criteria. The approach used for
extracting each block is the following:
1. Extraction of Candidate Gender Interactions
Prior to beginning the process of conducting the Bechdel-Wallace Test analysis, the model locates
potential gender interactions among the named female characters in the film. A key filter has been
applied to ensure that the model correctly isolates such interactions. First, the model must locate
specifically named women (excluding generic labels such as "waitress", "woman in crowd", etc.).
Second, the model must also identify the exact lines of dialogue shared between two female characters
not talking about a man. These strict filters enable the system to differentiate between simply mentioning
a woman and a true exchange between two women.
2. Extraction of Evidence of LGBTQ+ Representation

--- PAGE 53 ---
41
In representing LGBTQ+ identities through cinematic portrayals, there have historically existed
different methods of representation. However, for the purposes of this research, which relies upon data-
driven methodology, the method of extracting evidence of LGBTQ+ identities is strictly focused on
precision. As a result, only those interactions that represent explicit identity statements (for example, a
character publicly comes out), explicit consensual same-sex relationships or explicit expressions of
same-sex desire are included. Additionally, to prevent false positives being identified in the pipeline,
the model has been designed to remove "queer coding" based on stereotypes as well as any
humoristic/insultive/coercion that does not constitute authentic representation.
By being this strict, we ensure that only true positive LGBTQ+ representation is captured, at the cost of
not capturing more implicit, subjective interpretations.
3. Localization and Filtering of Physical Violence and Explicit Sexuality
In contrast to identifying every reference to a gun or suggestive comments, this module seeks to establish
where in the film there exist stages of physical violence and/or explicit sexuality that have sufficient
importance to be visually represented and/or to impact the narrative direction of the story. So, rather
than documenting every reference to weapons or suggestive language, the model is designed to
document only those scenes that show violence/sexuality in a manner that is important to the story. An
example of what the model would consider to be a distinction could include documenting a character
who discusses their experience during wartime as opposed to an actual fight scene that occurs on-screen.
Therefore, by making this distinction, the model ensures that the final dataset accurately represents both
the visual and narrative significance of physical violence and explicit sex throughout the film.
4. Identification of Socio-Technical Contextual Elements
As a part of the “Cinema as a mirror of society” goal, this component documents elements associated
with the social-technological environment. Examples of these environments include:
● Social and Class Conflicts: specific references to either economic disparity or labor concerns.
● Institutions: how the state, religious institutions and educational institutions interact with the
characters.
● Technology and AI: The inclusion of digital lives, robotics, or scientific advancements.
● Environment: References to environmental issues like climate change.
5. Historical and Political References
This module was created with one purpose in mind: To create an opportunity to integrate the movie's
storyline into actual history and to keep from using the models outside information or to use its own
interpretation. Evidence will be collected in the module only if the screenplay has the name of a
particular conflict or war, or the exact date of an event.
As stated above there is also an advantage of being able to have high accuracy as well as avoiding the
possibility of having the model invent some type of background based on other factors. Following are
some other types of criteria we used for accuracy:
● Specific Conflicts/Wars/Etc.: The model will identify a specific war/conflict/event if it can find
the specific conflict/war/event in either a dialog or a scene heading. For example;
WW2/Vietnam War/The moon landing etc... A generic reference to soldiers fighting in trenches
will not be enough. For example, if the script states that soldiers are in trenches during World
War II then the model would identify that as a specific historical event.

Appendix H: Rule-based Prechecks
The following prechecks were used to detect signals and validate consistency, but they were not used to
sustitute the LLM:
● Narrative Markers: Identification of non-linear storytelling and/or specific genre-related cues
(flashback, flashforward...)
● Thematic Keywords: Identification of explicit mention of war, drug use, physical disabilities,
etc. (cocaine, WWII, Vietnam...)
● Institutional Markers: Identification of terms related to governmental institutions, corporations,
or law enforcement agencies. (police, ACAB, corruption...)
Appendix I: Validation Logic by feature
1. Bechdel Verification Logic
A test that is both critical and fragile. To assure the reliability of its output, the data processing pipeline
has a verification step, which verifies a set of very strict criteria: The two females must be named in the
script. They must interact with each other directly. And the dialogue extracted must show clearly that

--- PAGE 55 ---
43
they are talking about something other than a man. In case these evidences do not agree, then the system
will override the model’s verdict to avoid false positives. Since in some movies the evidence is so subtle
and not important to the plot, we conducted the analysis taking into account that the model wouldn’t be
able to identify all the Bechdel Positive scripts.
2. LGBTQ Validation Logic
The system has very strict validating evidence that will eliminate all LGBTQ related signals that could
potentially be inaccurate. Therefore, it will identify true representations versus unrepresentative text like
jokes or insults.
3. Protagonist Gender and Lead Structure Logic
The identification of the main character(s) within an ensemble or ambiguous script can also be difficult
for the model. Therefore, the pipeline uses a deterministic speaker distribution (counts and dialogue
share) that was generated by identifying each character’s percentage of total dialogue. The LLM then
compares its narrative section with the actual amount of dialogue per character, thereby refining the
determination of whether there are one or two leads, or if all characters qualify as equal leads.
4. Racial/Minority Representation Logic
Race and minority representation is handled by the pipeline in a very conservative way. The majority
of screenplays do not include an explicit description of race, therefore the model will not make a
determination based on name or cultural archetype. The race and minority representation categories are
only populated if there is textual support provided (in character descriptions or specific plot point
references), otherwise the model assumes this to be "unknown", preventing speculative data entry into
these fields. This conservative approach is the reason why almost all scripts have “unknown” in the
protagonist's race field.
5. Institutions/Historical Logic
At this stage, the normalized presence of institutions/historical settings are established. The system cross
checks the resulting JSON against the determinative prechecks (presence of Government, Corporate,
Military etc. keywords) for consistency. If the LLM determines the theme is "War", however no
supporting historical or institutional markers were identified during the script scan process, then either
the category is refined or marked accordingly to ensure that the labels accurately reflect the content of
the story.

Appendix K: Streamlit Dashboard Details
The Streamlit Dashboard has been divided into multiple analytical areas:
● The general overview section displays basic information regarding the database, including total
movies analyzed, years covered in the database, decades found in the database, Bechdel pass
rate and rate of unknown values across features among many other information
● The social evolution section focuses on representation related aspects. It illustrates Bechdel test,
female dialogue share, protagonist gender, LGBTQ+ presence, explicit racial diversity,
economic struggle representation... These graphs illustrate broad changes over time in social
representation without requiring any interpretation.
● The narrative structure section analyzes how storytelling aspects change over time. This area
includes charts illustrating genre evolution through time, plot complexity over time, non-linear
narrative through time, opening types throughout history, ending types throughout history...
Allowing for comparative analysis of narrative structure among decades and genres.
● The violence, morality, and tone section illustrate violence intensity through time, type of
violence used throughout history, consequences of violence throughout history... Allowing to
cross-compare related features and their correlation.
● The institutional/political/historical section contains graphs like law enforcement representation
throughout history, government representation throughout history, corporate power
representations throughout history, war references throughout history...
● The technology and future section address environmental concerns, artificial intelligence
representations throughout history, digital revolution representations throughout history, human
vs technology conflict representations throughout history; scientific progress portrayals
throughout history; and representations of technology & future oriented themes and how those
are related to ending tones. An additional view is provided specifically for science fiction
movies where AI/technology/science/future oriented theme representations can be analyzed
together.
● The cross-analysis section contains graphs and figures that mix different analysis blocks,
providing relations between features that may seem like they’re not related. Some examples
include Technology versus Dark/ Disturbing endings or economic struggle and ending tone.
● The Explorer section allows us to create custom graphs by choosing the graph type and which
features to use in each axis.
● The Films section allow us to inspect the analysis result of a single film and see all the features.
● The Data section provides a helpful insight of the final dataset, so we can see how data is stored
